## Why this matters on consults

Psychotic symptoms in children and adolescents often first come to attention in pediatric, emergency, or outpatient settings rather than in specialty psychiatry care. The immediate task is usually not to name a final diagnosis, but to sort safety concerns, clarify the differential, identify medical or substance-related contributors, and decide how urgently the patient needs psychiatric follow-up or hospitalization.

## What counts as psychosis?

Psychosis is more than simply “hearing voices” or “seeing things.” A practical working definition includes:

- hallucinations, especially auditory verbal hallucinations
- delusions, including fixed false beliefs or bizarre/paranoid interpretations
- formal thought disorder, such as tangentiality, derailment, illogicality, perseveration, or thought blocking
- disorganized behavior or speech, including unclear thought processes or behavior that suggests loss of contact with reality

Hostility, agitation, or bizarre behavior can occur in psychosis, but those signs are not specific and can also appear in mood disorders, trauma-related states, intoxication, delirium, developmental conditions, or other medical illnesses.

## Primary versus secondary psychosis

It is helpful to separate **primary psychotic disorders** from **secondary psychosis**.

Primary psychotic disorders are illnesses in which psychosis is directly linked to a psychiatric condition, such as schizophrenia-spectrum illness or mood disorders with psychotic features.

Secondary psychosis refers to psychotic symptoms that arise from another cause, such as intoxication, withdrawal, delirium, encephalopathy, medication effect, or another medical disorder. In acute settings, this distinction matters because substance-related and medically secondary psychosis may be more common than a first presentation of schizophrenia.

## Differential diagnosis

### Primary psychiatric causes

- schizophrenia
- schizoaffective disorder
- bipolar disorder with psychotic features
- major depressive disorder with psychotic features
- brief psychotic disorder
- schizophreniform disorder
- PTSD with secondary psychotic features

When considering schizophrenia-spectrum illness, it helps to remember that psychosis alone is not the whole picture. Schizophrenia also includes negative symptoms such as blunted affect, alogia, avolition, anhedonia, and decreased social drive.

### Secondary and non-primary causes

- cannabis and other substance use
- stimulants, including amphetamines and methamphetamine
- synthetic cannabinoids or other synthetic substances
- alcohol
- PCP, ketamine, LSD, diphenhydramine, and related agents
- encephalitis or other inflammatory brain processes
- brain tumors or other lesions
- metabolic or endocrine disorders
- medication-induced states, including steroids, stimulants, or anticholinergics

## Red flags for urgent escalation

The following features should raise concern for urgent psychiatric referral, emergency evaluation, or hospitalization:

- suicidal or homicidal ideation
- severe aggression or agitation with concern for physical safety
- catatonia or extreme withdrawal with inability to care for self
- concern for medical instability
- very young age
- rapidly progressive, unusual, or neurologically concerning symptoms

## Referral and early intervention

Suspected new-onset primary psychosis should not be managed in isolation. Psychiatric referral is appropriate for any patient in whom a schizophrenia-spectrum illness or other primary psychotic disorder is being considered, and the goal is usually urgent follow-up within days rather than weeks.

Depending on local resources, good referral targets include:

- a local psychiatrist
- an academic medical center
- a dedicated early psychosis or early intervention program

Early intervention matters. Better long-term outcomes are associated with shorter duration of untreated psychosis, better family and social support, and stronger engagement with treatment. Worse outcomes are linked to prolonged untreated illness, persistent negative symptoms, comorbid substance use, and poor adherence.

## Initial treatment framing

Untreated psychosis can be harmful, so timely intervention matters. If a schizophrenia-spectrum illness is suspected, antipsychotic treatment may be appropriate, usually in close coordination with psychiatry. Medication choice is only one part of care. Side effects and monitoring requirements are substantial, and family education, alliance-building, and treatment adherence are central to good outcomes.

For many youth, initial treatment planning should also include:

- psychoeducation for the patient and family
- close attention to substance use
- coordination with therapy, social work, and school supports when appropriate
- recreation of “early intervention” supports locally when a formal program is unavailable

## Role of the consult team or medical home

The PCP, pediatric team, or consult psychiatrist often plays an important role even after psychiatry referral is made. Key contributions include:

- early recognition of psychotic symptoms
- initial safety assessment
- screening for substance use and obvious medical contributors
- helping families understand why urgent follow-up matters
- encouraging adherence and ongoing engagement with treatment
- partnering with psychiatry on monitoring and longitudinal care
